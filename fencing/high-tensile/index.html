High Tensile Fence Installation

High tensile is the lowest cost per foot of the permanent wire fences, and the only one that can be re-tensioned twenty years later with a handle instead of a rebuild. It is also the one that punishes a bad end assembly fastest. Call [PHONE].

What high tensile actually is

The wire is the difference. High tensile is typically 12.5 gauge, Class 3 galvanized, at roughly twice the tensile strength of the soft low carbon wire used in standard barbed wire.

That single property changes the whole system:

  • It holds tension instead of stretching out. Soft wire pulled hard yields and stays yielded. High tensile springs back.
  • It does not sag between posts, so line posts become spacers rather than structure.
  • It can be tensioned to a measured number and checked against that number later.
  • It stores real energy. A tensioned high tensile wire that breaks recoils hard. It is handled accordingly, and it is one reason this is not a weekend build.

Smooth or barbed?

Smooth high tensile is the standard. It is the only sensible choice if the fence is electrified, because the animal has to touch a conductor and back off, not get tangled in one. It is also the safer wire around horses, where barbs are the wrong answer regardless of wire type.

Barbed high tensile exists and it behaves nothing like soft barbed wire. It holds tension and it does not forgive. On a soft wire fence, an animal hitting it stretches the wire and the fence absorbs some of the impact. On barbed high tensile there is very little give, so the impact goes into the animal and into the end assembly. It has a place on hard boundary line where nothing should be leaning on it. It is a poor choice inside a working operation.

Most jobs are smooth, and most smooth jobs end up with at least one hot wire.

Post spacing, and why the cost per foot drops

Because the wire carries its own tension, line posts are not holding the wire up. They are holding it in position. That means:

  • Line post spacing commonly runs 20 to 30 feet on even ground in trade practice, against 12 to 16 for barbed wire, with battens or droppers between to hold strand spacing.
  • Fewer posts means fewer holes, less drilling, less concrete where concrete is used, fewer staples or insulators, and a faster build.
  • The material saving is in posts and labor, not in wire.

Iowa State University Extension's fence cost comparison (publication B1-75) ranks high tensile the lowest annual cost per foot of the permanent wire fence types it compares, ahead of woven wire and barbed wire, mainly on that post count and on service life. Those are Iowa figures at an Iowa labor rate. Use them for ranking the types against each other, not as a price in this market.

Spacing tightens for the same reasons it tightens on any fence: grade breaks, crests, dips, curves, and wherever stock puts steady pressure on the line.

In-line strainers and tensioners

A strainer is a ratcheting tension device set into the run of the wire, operated with a handle. It is the reason a high tensile fence can be brought back to working tension years later with a handle instead of being rebuilt.

What it buys you:

  • Re-tensioning without rebuilding. Wire relaxes over time, ground shifts, a tree comes down. With strainers in the line you walk the fence with a handle and put the tension back. Without them, restoring tension means dropping the run and re-pulling to the end assembly.
  • Repair in place. A broken strand gets spliced and re-tensioned at the strainer.
  • Control of individual strands. The bottom wire and the top wire do not carry the same load and do not need the same tension.

How we place them:

  • One strainer per strand per manageable run, not one per mile. A strainer can only pull the length of wire it can move.
  • Located where a person can reach them without a ladder or a fight through brush, because a strainer nobody can get to never gets used.
  • Set back from corners, not crowded into the assembly.

Spring gauges

A spring gauge is an in-line tension spring with a scale on it. It does two separate jobs and both matter.

It tells you the tension you actually have. Not the tension you think you have. Trade practice commonly targets somewhere in the range of 150 to 250 pounds per strand, and the right number varies with the wire, the span, the terrain, and what you are containing. Without a gauge, "tight" is an opinion and every strand ends up different.

It absorbs shock. An animal hits the wire, a limb drops on it, snow loads it, or the temperature drops thirty degrees overnight and the whole fence contracts. The spring takes that spike and gives it back, instead of the wire snapping or the end assembly being pulled out of the ground.

A high tensile fence with no gauge anywhere in it is a fence built on guesswork.

If it is electrified

An electric fence is a psychological barrier on a physical frame. Both halves have to be right.

Insulators. The wire must not touch wood, steel, or ground anywhere along the hot strands. Line post insulators on every hot wire. Strain insulators at ends and corners, where the wire dead-ends and where the voltage will find a path if it can. If the end assembly itself is not insulated, the whole run is shorted at the corner and the fence reads dead with the energizer working perfectly.

Grounding is the half people skip. The circuit runs through the animal and back through the soil to the ground system. An undersized ground field is the single most common reason an electric fence does not bite. That means multiple ground rods, driven their full length, spaced well apart, and joined by a continuous ground wire, not one short rod behind the barn.

Dry soil changes the design. The Inland Northwest gets dry and stays dry through late summer, and dry soil is a poor return path. Where that is the case the fence is built as a hot and ground return system, alternating charged and grounded wires, so the animal completes the circuit by touching two wires instead of relying on the soil.

Layout. Hot wires go where the animal makes contact, which is nose height for the species in question. A hot wire six inches off the correct height teaches the animal nothing.

The end assemblies decide everything

This is the part to read twice.

Every strand of tension in a high tensile fence is dead-ended into a corner or end assembly. There are fewer posts than any other fence type, the wire is pulled harder than any other fence type, and none of that load is being shared with the line posts. It all lands in the assemblies.

So this is the one fence type where bad bracing shows up fast.

  • On a barbed wire fence, an undersized brace leans slowly over a few seasons and you lose tension gradually.
  • On a woven wire fence, the corner leans and the sheet relaxes.
  • On a high tensile fence, an undersized brace starts moving in the first season. The moment it moves, every strand tied to it goes slack at once, over the entire run, because there is nothing else holding the wire up. A slack high tensile fence is a set of hanging wires. It contains nothing.

What the assemblies get:

  • Larger diameter posts than line posts, set deep, and set plumb.
  • Horizontal brace rails long enough to work. Trade practice runs the horizontal roughly two to two and a half times the height of the post above ground.
  • Double H assemblies at high strand counts, long pulls, soft ground, and at every corner turning a hard angle.
  • The diagonal wrapped and twisted to real tension with a twitch, then re-checked after the run is pulled.
  • Strain insulators at the assembly on any electrified run.

Detail lives on braces and corners. If you are comparing bids on a high tensile job, compare the assemblies and the strainer count. The wire is the same wire in every bid.

When high tensile is not the right call

We will say so rather than sell it:

  • Short runs with many corners. The savings are in post count over distance. A short run with four corners is mostly assemblies, and the economics disappear.
  • Small paddocks and dry lots. Animals stand against the fence all day. That wants field and woven wire or rail, not wire on 25 foot spacing.
  • Nobody to maintain it. High tensile is a system that rewards a walk-through once a season. If the fence is going to be ignored for a decade, a heavier passive fence is the better buy.
  • Horses in a small space. See horse and paddock fencing. Smooth high tensile with visible sight rails or tape can work on large pasture. Bare wire in a paddock does not.
