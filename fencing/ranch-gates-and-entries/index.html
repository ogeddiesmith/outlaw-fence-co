Ranch Gates, Entries and Cattle Guards

Gates are the part of a fence you touch. Everything else you look at once a year and forget about. The gate gets used in the dark, in the rain, with gloves on, with a trailer behind you and something in your other hand.

They are also the part of a fence that shows. The entry is the first thing anyone sees of a place, and a sagging gate at the road tells a story about everything behind it, fairly or not.

Tube gate or wire gap?

A wire gap, the gap made out of the fence wire itself with a stay on the end and loops over the post, has exactly one thing going for it: it is nearly free, because it is made out of what you already have on the fence.

Everything else about it is a cost you pay later. It takes two hands and some strength. In cold weather it takes more. It gets left open because closing it is unpleasant. It sags out of shape. It is the weakest point in an otherwise sound fence, and once it has been dropped in the mud twice it stops even being that.

A wire gap is the right call for a back fence line you cross twice a year with a four wheeler.

Anywhere used weekly, or used by anybody other than you, gets a hung tube gate. Steel frame, hinged, latched, opens with one hand, works in gloves, stays square. Neighbors, hired help, a hay crew, a vet and a fuel truck should never be asked to work a wire gap. They will leave it open eventually and it will be nobody's fault but the design's.

Gate width: why 16 feet

The tractor is not the widest thing you pull through a gate. The implement is, and the implement is behind you where you cannot see it.

Set that aside, though, because the real argument for width is not the equipment. It is the angle.

A 12 foot gate gives you a 12 foot target you have to hit square. Come at it fifteen degrees off, which is what happens when the lane bends, or the ground is soft, or you are coming downhill, or there is a stack of round bales in the way, and the effective opening is narrower than the gate. You are threading a trailer through a hole that just got smaller, in reverse, in the mud.

A 16 foot gate lets an operator come in off an angle without lining up perfectly, and lets a driver correct instead of stopping, backing up and trying again. That is the entire value of the extra four feet, and it gets returned every time somebody comes through the gate in a hurry.

Practical rules:

  • Main equipment entry: 16 feet minimum. This is the gate on the lane, the hayfield, the pasture the tractor works.
  • 20 feet, or a pair of gates making 24, where big equipment or a truck turns in. Two 12 foot leaves also let you open half of it for a pickup.
  • 12 or 14 feet is fine for a paddock or a pen you only take a truck and a small trailer into.
  • A walk gate beside a big gate is worth its cost anywhere people go on foot, because a 16 foot leaf swung open for one person is a nuisance and it gets left open.
  • Measure the widest thing that comes to the place, add real clearance both sides, and then round up to the next standard size. Gates come in fixed increments, so rounding up is usually cheap.

And a gate is more than its leaf. The opening has to be square, the ground on both sides flat enough to stop, swing and pull through, and the swing arc clear of the fence line, the ditch and anything you park.

Hanging a heavy gate so it does not sag

Almost every dragging gate in the county has the same cause: the post.

A gate is a cantilever. All of its weight hangs off one edge, held by two hinges, and the two hinges do opposite things. The top hinge is in tension, pulling the top of the post toward the gate. The bottom hinge is in compression, pushing the base of the post away from the gate. Between them they are trying to rotate the post out of the ground, every hour of every day, plus whatever shock arrives when somebody swings the gate hard into the stop.

So:

The hinge post is not a line post. It is an end post carrying an extra moment. Common practice is 8 inch top diameter wood, or heavy pipe, set around 4 feet deep, in concrete or tamped rock, depending on soil. Nobody has ever regretted an oversized gate post.

It has to be braced back into the fence line, with the assembly running away from the opening. That is a full braced assembly, not a token one, and it is exactly the work described on braces and corners. A gate post with no brace behind it will lean toward the opening no matter how deep it went.

Hinge pins point opposite ways. Top pin down, bottom pin up. A gate hung with both pins the same way can be lifted off its hinges, by a person or by an animal working underneath it. This costs nothing and it is skipped constantly.

Use adjustable hinges on anything heavy. Threaded bolt-through hinges let the gate be re-plumbed later without pulling the post. Ground moves. Wood shrinks. Being able to take up a quarter inch in five minutes is the difference between a gate that lasts and a gate that gets a wheel screwed to it.

Hang it with a hair of rise on the latch side. A gate hung dead level ends up low. Hung slightly high, it settles into level.

A gate wheel is a symptom, not a fix, on anything under about 16 feet. On a very long leaf on soft ground it is legitimate. On a 12 foot gate it means the post was undersized.

Never hang a real gate on a T-post or a 4 inch line post. It will work for one season.

Decide which way it swings before the posts go in. Into the pasture where cattle push against it. Both ways where you sort. Away from the prevailing drift if snow piles on that side, because a gate that opens into a drift is a shovel job all winter.

Latches that work in gloves

A latch that needs a fingertip is a latch that fails in January.

What actually works:

  • Chain and hook. Universal, cheap, forgiving of a gate that is slightly out of square, and it works with gloves on. Keep the chain fixed to the post so it does not get carried off, and keep it long enough to reach without a fight.
  • Self-catching and slam latches. The gate closes and catches without a free hand. This is the right latch for anybody working horseback or carrying buckets.
  • Two-way lever latches on sorting gates, so the gate can be worked from either side.
  • Reachable from both sides, at a height reachable from the ground, from a truck window if that is how the gate gets used, and from horseback if that is how the place is run.
  • A positive stop on any gate driven through in the dark, so the gate catches at the post instead of swinging past into the side of the truck.

Two more worth knowing. Horses open latches. A horse with time on its hands will work a simple lever open, so a horse gate wants a latch a horse cannot flip, plus a chain as backup. And a latch that only works when the gate is perfectly square is a latch that stops working the first time the post settles. Pick the forgiving one.

Entries and overhead structures

The entrance is the piece of the property that gets looked at most and built worst.

An overhead entry is two heavy uprights, usually pipe set in concrete, a top beam, and a name panel hung or welded to it. Straightforward to build, and there are four things that decide whether it works or becomes a problem:

Clear height is about the tallest load that comes to the place, not the owner's pickup. A stacked hay truck, a horse trailer with a tall roof, a feed delivery, a livestock pot. Measure the tallest thing that has ever come down that lane and build for more than that. An entry that has to be driven around is an expensive ornament, and lowering a beam is easy while raising one is not.

Clear width has to pass a trailer coming off the county road at an angle, which is a tighter geometry problem than it looks on paper.

Wind load on the sign panel is a real structural load. A large flat panel between two uprights is a sail. That is why entry uprights get concrete and depth that would be overkill for the same post in a fence line.

Set the gate back from the road. Far enough that a truck and trailer can pull entirely clear of the travel lane while somebody gets out to open it. This is a safety item and it is the one owners regret most often, because moving an entry after the fact means moving everything.

Simpler entries work too: a pair of heavy uprights with no beam, a stone or timber column each side, or just an oversized hung gate hung dead square. The most common mistake at an entry is not being too plain. It is being undersized.

Cattle guards

A cattle guard is a gate that nobody has to open, which is why they get put on a lane the moment the traffic justifies it.

Most people specify the grid and forget the pit, and the pit is what determines whether it still works in five years.

The grid. Load rating matters, because at some point a loaded truck, a fuel delivery or a fire apparatus is going to cross it, and a guard rated for a pickup is not rated for that. Width has to match the road with room to spare, since a trailer wandering off the edge of a guard is a bad afternoon. Length across, in the direction of travel, has to be long enough that an animal cannot step or jump it, and a guard that is too short is simply a noise strip.

The pit is the part that fails. A cattle guard works because there is open air under the grid. As soon as the pit fills with dirt, gravel and washed-in sediment, the grid is sitting on solid ground and cattle walk over it like a rough patch of road. So the pit needs real depth, a way for water to leave it (a drain, or grade out one end), and an owner who knows it is a clean-out item. A guard that has never been cleaned out is usually a guard that stopped working a while ago.

The approaches. The grid bears on footings, not on loose soil, and the road on each side has to come up level with the grid. Settled approaches are the most common complaint about any cattle guard: the ramp sinks, the grid stands proud, and every trailer takes a hit crossing it. Getting the footings and the compaction right at install is what avoids that.

Wing fences. The fence has to tie into both ends of the guard so nothing simply walks around the end. Skipping this makes the whole thing decorative.

What a cattle guard will not do. It will not reliably hold horses, and many designs will not hold sheep or goats. Plan a gate beside the guard for horses, for foot traffic and for the days you need the lane closed to everything.
