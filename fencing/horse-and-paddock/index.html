Horse and Paddock Fencing

A fence that holds cattle can badly injure a horse. If you are fencing a horse property, the spec is different from the first post to the last latch. Call [PHONE].

Horses fail a fence differently than cattle

This is the whole design problem, and it is worth stating plainly before any material gets picked.

Behavior Cattle Horses
Contact speed Walk into it, lean, push Run into it at speed, sometimes in a panic
Response to pressure Stop and back off Push through, or keep going
Legs Stay under the animal Paw, strike, and go through openings
Reaction to being caught Stand and wait Fight it until something breaks
Chewing Rare Cribbing and chewing wood is common
Eyesight of thin wire Not a factor at walking pace Nearly invisible at a gallop

Two consequences follow from that table and they drive every choice below.

A horse must be able to see the fence. Most horse fence failures at speed are visibility failures, not strength failures.

A horse must not be able to get a leg through it. A caught leg is where the serious injuries happen, and it is where a repairable fence becomes a destroyed fence.

Why barbed wire is a poor horse fence

Direct answer, because people ask:

  • A horse that hits barbed wire does not stop the way a cow does. It keeps moving through the wire.
  • Barbs cut, and then the wire holds the wound open while the horse fights it. The injuries are lacerations and degloving, not scrapes.
  • The wire is thin and unlit against the ground. At speed a horse does not see it.
  • A hoof through a barbed strand catches, and the horse cannot back out of a barb.

Barbed wire is a legitimate cattle and boundary fence. On a horse property it belongs on the far boundary line at most, if at all, and never on a paddock, a turnout, or anywhere horses run.

No-climb and 2x4 mesh

This is the standard horse fabric and it is the default recommendation on most horse jobs.

  • Opening size is 2 inches by 4 inches. That is the point. A hoof does not pass through a 2 inch by 4 inch opening, so the leg never gets caught and the horse never fights the fence.
  • The fabric is uniform, not graduated. No large squares anywhere on the sheet, top to bottom, because a horse can reach any part of it.
  • Fixed knot or woven. Fixed knot fabric holds its square shape under steady leaning, which is exactly what happens along a fence line where the grass is better on the other side. See field and woven wire for the joint comparison.
  • V-mesh is the diamond-pattern variant of the same idea. Same logic, tighter geometry, higher price.
  • The bottom sits close to grade. A horse that can get a hoof under the fabric will roll under it or paw at it.

No-climb has one weakness on its own: it is nearly invisible from a distance, and the top edge of the fabric is a thin wire at chest height. That is what the next section is for.

Sight lines: the rail on top of the mesh

Mesh plus a top rail is the single most common upgrade on a horse fence and it is the one worth the money.

The rail does three jobs at once:

  1. Gives the horse something to see at eye level from across the paddock, at speed, in low light.
  2. Caps the top edge of the mesh so a leaning horse pushes against a rail instead of rolling the top wire over.
  3. Stiffens the whole fence and gives the run a finished line, which is also what makes the property look like a horse property.

The rail can be wood, vinyl, pipe, or a flexible polymer rail. All four work. The choice is maintenance and budget, not safety, once the mesh is behind it.

On larger pasture where mesh is not economical, sight is provided by a top board, a wide poly tape, or an electric rope with visible strands. A bare smooth wire, high tensile or not, is not a sight line.

Board fence and ranch rail

Classic, and still correct in the right place.

  • Three or four rails, typically to a 4 foot 6 inch to 5 foot top rail in trade practice.
  • Boards go on the inside of the posts. A horse leaning from inside pushes the board into the post, so the post carries the load. Boards on the outside are held on by nothing but fasteners, and a leaning horse pops them off.
  • Chewing and cribbing are the maintenance line item. Horses eat board fence. The answers are a metal cap rail on the top board, a hot wire offset on the inside face, or a coated product. Budget for one of them at build time, not after the top rail is gone.
  • Board fence does not contain a foal or a determined horse on its own. A horse goes through the gap between rails. On foaling paddocks and small turnouts, board fence gets backed with mesh.
  • Ongoing cost is real. Paint or stain, replacement boards, and nails backing out. It is the best-looking fence and the highest maintenance fence.

Vinyl and coated options

  • Vinyl rail looks like board fence, does not rot, does not need paint, and flexes rather than splintering on impact. That flex is the selling point and also the limit: a horse that leans hard can pop a rail out of the post. On its own, vinyl rail is a visual barrier plus a light physical one. On a horse that tests fence, back it with mesh or a hot wire on the inside face.
  • PVC-coated wire mesh adds thickness and visibility to the fabric and reduces cut risk on contact. Costs more, lasts longer against corrosion.
  • Coated high tensile and poly-coated wire raise the visibility of a wire fence considerably, which is the actual problem being solved. See high tensile fencing for how that system is tensioned.
  • Flexible polymer rail is a wide, high-visibility rail under tension. It gives on impact and returns. It needs proper end assemblies because it is a tensioned system, not a nailed one.

Height

Trade practice, and it varies with the horse:

Situation Typical top height
General turnout and pasture perimeter 4 feet 6 inches to 5 feet
Small paddock or dry lot, horses close to the fence all day 5 feet
Stallions, breeding stock, or a horse with a jumping habit 6 feet
Foaling paddock 5 feet, with mesh to grade and no gap a foal can roll under

Height is not the main safety variable. The opening size and the sight line are. A 6 foot fence a horse cannot see is worse than a 4 foot 6 inch fence it can.

Corners, gates, and hardware

This is where horses get hurt on otherwise good fences.

  • No sharp interior angles. A dominant horse will corner a subordinate one, and a tight acute corner is a trap with no exit. Cut the corner off with a short diagonal panel, round it, or fill it. The horse needs a way out.
  • No projecting hardware at horse height. No bolt ends sticking out, no exposed lag heads, no cut wire tails, no T-post caps at eye level, no fastener that can hook a halter or a blanket.
  • Gate latches horses cannot operate. Horses learn slide bolts. Two-action latches or a chain and snap are the practical answers, and they have to be usable one-handed while leading a horse.
  • No gaps at the hinge side. A hoof or a head in a hinge gap is the same injury as a hoof through a fence.
  • Gate bottoms sit close to grade for the same reason the fabric does.
  • Width. Trade practice is roughly 4 feet for a walk gate, and 12 to 16 feet for anything a tractor, truck, or trailer has to get through. Measure your widest piece of equipment before we set the posts, not after.

Paddock versus pasture

Same property, two different fences, and the bid should say so.

A paddock or dry lot takes far more abuse per foot than a pasture, because the horse is never more than a few strides from the fence. Spec it heavier: closer post spacing, mesh with a top rail, and often a hot wire offset on the inside face to keep horses off the fabric entirely.

A pasture perimeter can run lighter and longer. Wider post spacing, mesh where it is affordable, and rails or visible tensioned rail where it is not.

Spending paddock money on the whole perimeter is how a horse fence budget disappears. Spending pasture money on the paddock is how the paddock fence fails first.

Bracing does not get skipped on a horse fence

A horse fence is under tension like any other wire fence, and mesh loads the corner across its full height. Undersized corner and end assemblies lean, the fabric relaxes, the top edge rolls, and then you have a loose sheet of wire that a horse can push a leg through. The assemblies are the fence.

The typical job here is small acreage

Most horse fencing in this region is not on a ranch. It is on a lifestyle property of five to forty acres, with two to six horses, a paddock near the barn and a larger turnout beyond it.

The land data says the same thing. Across the state line in Kootenai County, 69 percent of farms are under 50 acres, and there are roughly 0.38 horses for every head of cattle, which is about double the ratio in Spokane County (USDA 2022 Census of Agriculture). That is horse and hobby-farm country, not cattle country.

What that means for a bid: the runs are shorter, the corner count per foot is higher, the fence is looked at every day from the house, and the finish matters as much as the containment. We price those jobs as what they are, not as a discount version of a pasture fence.
