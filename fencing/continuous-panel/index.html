Continuous Fence Panel

Continuous fence is welded steel panel, run in long sections, bolted to a post at each joint. There is no wire, nothing to stretch, and nothing to re-tension. Each panel is a rigid beam that stands on its own.

That is the whole idea, and it is why it goes where wire keeps failing.

Do not confuse it with a portable corral panel. Portable panels are freestanding and they move when cattle hit them. Continuous fence is a permanent fence that happens to be built out of panel sections instead of strands.

What a panel is

Spec Typical
Panel length 20 feet, some manufacturers at 24
Rails 4, 5, 6 or 7
Rail material Round tube, commonly around 1.3 to 1.7 inch outside diameter, or flat punched rail
Overall height Roughly 48 inches at 4 rail up to around 6 feet at 7 rail. Varies by manufacturer
Finish Painted, or galvanized at higher cost
Connection Lapped at a post and bolted, or clamped with panel brackets

Rail count is the decision that drives everything else, and it is a containment question, not a looks question.

  • 4 rail holds mature cattle on a boundary or a lot where they are not crowded.
  • 5 rail is the common all-purpose choice for corrals and traffic areas.
  • 6 or 7 rail is for calves, small stock, bulls, and anywhere you need the bottom tight to the ground so nothing goes under.

Bottom rail height off the ground matters more than people expect. A calf goes under a fence long before it goes through one.

Gauge and wall thickness of the tube is the other spec that separates a cheap panel from one that survives a bull leaning on it, and it is worth asking about on any quote, ours or anybody else's.

How it connects, and how corners are made

Along a straight run: panels are lapped over each other at a post and bolted through, or held with panel clamps. The post carries the joint. Posts are typically driven pipe, commonly 2 3/8 or 2 7/8 inch, though wood is used where the look calls for it. Because each panel spans its full length, you need far fewer posts than a wire fence over the same distance, and post spacing is set by panel length rather than by wire sag.

At a corner: a corner post is set and the panels are cut to length and fixed to it at the angle. This is the part that gets done badly. A bolted lap can pivot slightly under load, so a corner made from a bolted lap alone will work loose and open up. A proper corner is welded, or it uses a bracket that fixes the angle mechanically. Ask any bidder how they make their corners.

At a gate: the opening gets framed with pipe posts set for the weight of the gate, the same way any hung gate is framed. See ranch gates and entries.

On rolling ground: panels are straight and rigid. They do not follow a contour the way wire does. Over gentle undulation the panels get stepped, which leaves a gap at the bottom on the high side, and on rougher ground panels get cut. That is real work and it is one of the reasons continuous fence costs more per foot than wire on uneven country.

The mechanical difference from a wire fence

This is the part that actually decides which product you want, and it comes down to how each one fails.

A wire fence works in tension. Every strand is anchored at its ends, and the entire run depends on those end assemblies. Cut one strand or move one corner and the whole span goes slack at once. That is why braces and corners is where the money and the skill go in wire work.

A continuous panel fence works in bending. Each panel carries its own load between its posts. Nothing is under tension, so nothing is stored waiting to release. A tractor that catches a panel bends that panel. The one beside it is unaffected. The repair is one 20 foot section, and it is a bolt-out and bolt-in, not a rebuild and re-stretch.

For an owner, that difference reads as: wire is cheaper to build and its failures are large. Panel is more expensive to build and its failures are small and local.

Where continuous panel beats wire

High-traffic areas. Around gates, water, feeders and mineral, where cattle stand and crowd. This is where wire fences wear out first and where panel pays for itself fastest.

Corrals, alleys and lots. Cattle push against a corral by design. Wire in a corral is a maintenance job forever. See corrals and cattle handling.

Bull pens and anywhere animals lean. A bull will find out what a fence is made of. Rigid steel does not stretch, does not sag, and does not teach him he can move it.

Road frontage and driveways. The stretch of fence everybody sees, including the appraiser, the buyer and the neighbor. It photographs well, it looks finished, and it does not sag between posts.

Horse paddocks where wire injury is the fear. No strands, no barbs, nothing to get a leg through. Rail spacing has to be chosen for horses, not cattle. See horse and paddock fencing.

Anywhere a wire fence has already failed twice. If the same 300 feet has been rebuilt more than once, the fence is not the problem. The product is wrong for that location.

Where it does not make sense

We would rather tell you this before you ask for a number on it.

A long boundary run across open ground. The cost per foot does not justify itself over a mile of pasture perimeter where nothing is crowding the fence. That is what woven wire and barbed wire are for. See field and woven wire and barbed wire.

Rough, rolling or broken terrain. Every contour change costs you either a gap at the bottom or a cut panel. Wire follows ground for free.

Where the fence needs to give. Snow load, drifting, and elk moving through are all cases where a fence that flexes and recovers beats a fence that bends and stays bent. A wire fence hit by an elk is usually a fence you re-tension. A panel hit by an elk is usually a panel you replace.

Long stretches you never look at and nothing ever leans on. Spending panel money there means less money for the parts that matter.

The right answer on most places is a mix: continuous panel on the frontage, the entry, the corrals and the traffic areas, wire on the back boundary. Putting the steel where it shows and where the pressure is is not a compromise. It is how the budget should be spent.

The speed advantage

A wire fence is built in stages: set every post along the line, hang the wire, stretch it, tie it off at braced assemblies, staple the whole run, then walk it back and adjust. A continuous panel fence goes up in 20 foot increments, post and panel, post and panel, and the run is finished behind the crew as it moves.

That has three consequences worth knowing:

  1. Less site disruption. The job is done in less time on the ground, which matters when the fence crosses a lane you use or a pen you need back.
  2. Fewer posts to set. Post setting is the slowest part of any fence, and panel needs a fraction of the posts wire needs over the same distance.
  3. It is often the cheapest way to buy a finished-looking result on a visible stretch, once you account for what your time is worth and what re-stretching wire on that stretch will cost over the next ten years.

We will not put a completion date on a job we have not walked, and anybody who quotes you a schedule before seeing the ground is guessing.

Maintenance and lifecycle

There is not much to it, which is the point.

  • No re-tensioning. Nothing is under load waiting to be tightened.
  • No staples to back out. Staples are the maintenance item on a wood-and-wire fence and they do not exist here.
  • Paint versus galvanized. Painted panel rusts first at the welds and at any cut end, so every cut end should be primed on install. Galvanized costs more up front and takes that maintenance off the table.
  • Damage is local. Bent rails can often be straightened in place. A destroyed section is unbolted and replaced without touching what is either side of it.

What drives the price

Continuous fence is quoted per foot, but the per-foot number moves a long way depending on:

  • Rail count and overall height
  • Tube diameter and wall thickness
  • Painted or galvanized
  • Post type, post spacing and how the posts go in, including whether we hit rock
  • Terrain and how many panels need cutting or stepping
  • Corners, since a properly made corner is fabrication, not a bolt
  • Gates and gate framing
  • Access, and how close a truck can get to the line
