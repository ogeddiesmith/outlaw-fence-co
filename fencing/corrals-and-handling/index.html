Corrals and Cattle Handling Systems

A corral is not a pen with gates in it. It is a piece of equipment, and it either works with the way cattle move or it fights them.

The difference shows up on the day you work them. A system that flows means two people, a morning, and cattle that walk into the chute. A system that does not means extra help, a whole day, hot cattle, a vet standing around on the clock, bruising, shrink, and somebody getting hurt in the crowd pen.

You buy the design once. You pay for a bad one every single time you work cattle, for as long as you own the place.

That is why we scope this work as a system tied to your cattle, your equipment and your ground, and not by the foot.

How cattle move, and what that means for the layout

Cattle are not stubborn. They are predictable, and most corral problems are the corral asking them to do something they will not do.

They want to go back where they came from. This is the single most useful fact in handling design. A curve exploits it: as an animal comes around the bend, it believes it is heading back toward the herd, so it moves forward willingly. A long straight alley shows it the dead end and the handler at the far end, and it stops.

They follow the leader. Move them in groups, never one at a time, and make sure the animal in front can see one ahead of it. That is the case for open, see-through sides in the holding pens and along the sorting alley.

They balk at what they can see. Shadows across the floor, a puddle, a change in footing color, a coat on a rail, a chain swinging, a person standing where they are supposed to go. Almost every "they will not load" problem is a visual one, and almost all of it is fixable at the design stage.

Solid sides where you want them moving. Open sides where you want them settling. The crowd pen, the alley and the approach to the chute get solid sides so the animal cannot see the handler, the truck, the dog, or the way out sideways. With nothing else to look at, forward is the only option. The holding pens stay open so cattle can see each other and stay calm.

They circle around a handler. Working position matters more than pressure. A walkway alongside the alley, at the right height, lets a person move along the line and pick up each animal's point of balance without climbing in with them.

Crowding tub or bud box?

Both work. They fail in different ways, which is what should decide it.

Crowding tub Bud box
What it is A curved crowd pen with a swinging gate that follows cattle around into the alley A small rectangular pen where cattle enter, turn back, and find the alley beside where they came in
Why it works The curve returns them toward where they came from They come back to their entry point and the alley is right there
Cost Higher. More steel, more fabrication Lower. Panels, posts and two gates
Forgiving of inexperienced help Yes, more so No. Rewards a quiet handler, punishes a loud one
Group size Handles larger draws Works best in small draws
Common failure Overfilled. A tub crammed full stops working Used as a holding pen. The box has to be empty before the next draw enters

The bud box is not cheap because it is a lesser thing. It is cheap because it is mostly empty space and correct geometry. But the geometry has to be right: the alley entrance sits beside the entry gate, the box is sized so cattle can turn without jamming, and it never holds cattle. Get any of that wrong and it is just a square pen that does not work.

If more than one person will ever run your cattle, or if you draw big, we will usually push you toward the tub. If it is you and one other person who both handle quietly, the box does the same job for less money and less to maintain.

The alley

The alley's job is to deliver a steady line of animals to the chute so the chute is never sitting empty and never being fed one at a time.

Width is set by the class of cattle, not by the material. Too wide and an animal turns around, which is the worst thing that can happen in an alley. Too narrow and they jam and bruise. Typical practice:

Class Typical alley width
Calves About 18 to 20 inches
Mature cows About 26 to 30 inches
Bulls About 30 to 32 inches
Mixed groups Tapered, roughly 16 inches at the bottom and 30 to 32 inches at the top

The tapered or V alley is the answer for anybody running mixed classes through the same system. The narrow bottom stops a calf from turning around while the wide top gives a mature cow room in the shoulders. If you run calves and cows through the same corral, this is the detail to spend on.

Length matters as much as width. An alley that holds only a couple of animals means somebody is bringing cattle up constantly and the chute is idle half the time. Enough alley to hold a working line is what makes the whole system fast.

Sides solid, walkway alongside. Solid to roughly shoulder height on the cattle, and a raised catwalk on one side so the handler works from above and beside, not in front.

Your chute is the customer. The pen has to serve it.

The chute and headgate are almost always the owner's own equipment, bought before we ever showed up, and often the most expensive single item on the place. The corral gets designed around it, not the other way around.

What we need to know before drawing anything:

  • Make and model of the chute, and manual or hydraulic. Hydraulic needs a power source and a properly drained, level pad. Manual needs clearance for the handle swing on the working side.
  • Inlet height and width, so the alley meets it flush. A step up, a step down, or a gap at the joint is a balk point every single time.
  • Level ground under it. A chute set on ground that is not flat racks slightly out of square, and a racked chute stops latching cleanly. That is the fault people blame on the chute for years.
  • Room behind it for the alley and a no-back or slide gate so the animal in the chute cannot be pushed or backed.
  • Room in front of it for release, and a clear path to where that animal is supposed to go next.
  • Where the sorting happens after release, and whether you need to cut two ways or three.
  • Light and power, if you ever work in the dark or early.
  • Where the vet stands, and where the vet parks.

Gates one person can operate

Most places are worked by one person more often than they will admit. A corral that requires two people to run costs the owner a favor every time.

What that means in the build:

  • Latches reachable from both sides of the fence, at a height reachable from the ground and from horseback.
  • One-handed latches. If it needs a fingertip and two hands, it fails in January and it fails with a bucket in the other hand.
  • Sorting gates that swing both ways and catch on their own, so the gate can be thrown and forgotten.
  • Gates hung so they do not drop as they swing, because a heavy gate that has to be lifted at the far end is a two-person gate. See ranch gates and entries for how heavy gates get hung.
  • Gates sized so the open gate becomes the alley wall. A gate that blocks a route when opened is doing two jobs and saving you a panel.
  • A way to tie or hook every gate in the open position so the wind does not close it behind you.

Man passes and escape gaps

You cannot outrun a cow, and the day it matters you will be in the crowd pen with your back to something.

Standard practice is a vertical slot in the pen wall, roughly 12 to 18 inches wide, that a person can turn sideways and step through and an animal cannot. They go in the crowd pen, along the alley, and anywhere a handler works on foot inside with stock, spaced so there is one within a couple of steps from any position a person will actually be standing.

Two rules. Never place a pass where an animal can get its head into it, because a head in a slot is an injured animal and a blocked exit. And a climb-over point is not an escape gap: a rail you have to get a boot onto is too slow when it counts.

Footing and drainage

Mud is what turns a good corral into a corral nobody uses in March.

Drainage is designed first, before the pens are laid out, because the layout can move and the water cannot. The working area gets crowned or sloped so water leaves it, and it is graded away from the chute, not toward it, since the chute area is where you and the vet stand and where the cattle are least able to move their feet.

The high-traffic ground gets a compacted base, usually gravel, in the places cattle stand, turn and crowd: the crowd pen, the alley, the chute approach and the gate openings. Those are the spots that churn first.

If any part of it is concrete, it has to be grooved. Slick concrete causes splayed legs, split animals and injured handlers, and a smooth pad in a working area is one of the more expensive mistakes you can pour.

Then it is worth walking the site in the wet season before we build, not the dry one. Everything drains fine in August.

Pipe, continuous panel or wood?

Material Where it earns its money The trade-off
Welded pipe Crowd pens, alleys, bull pens, anywhere cattle push hard, anywhere you want it permanent Highest labor cost, needs welding on site, hardest to modify later
Continuous panel Corral perimeter, holding pens, alleys, long straight sections Fast and consistent, less forgiving on rolling ground. See continuous panel
Wood Sorting and holding pens, budget builds, places that get modified Cheapest up front, gets chewed and broken, more maintenance
Portable panels Temporary setups, a system you are still figuring out Not a permanent corral. Moves when cattle hit it

Most working systems are a mix. Money goes into pipe where cattle push and into panel where you need length, and wood carries the pens that just hold stock. Putting pipe everywhere is a way to spend a lot on the parts nothing ever leans on.

How we scope a corral job

We come out and walk it. What gets asked:

  1. How many head, what class, and how many at a time.
  2. What you own already: chute, headgate, tub, panels, anything reusable.
  3. Who works the cattle and how many people are usually there.
  4. Where the cattle come from and where they go afterward.
  5. Where a truck and trailer can back in, turn around, and get out when it is wet.
  6. Where the water, the power and the drainage are.
  7. What goes wrong today. The owner already knows the balk points. That is the most valuable information on the site visit.
