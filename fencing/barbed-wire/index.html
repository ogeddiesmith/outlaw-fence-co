Barbed Wire Fence Installation

If you have a pasture line to build, a boundary to run, or a stretch of old wire gone slack, call [PHONE]. We walk the line before we quote it.

Barbed wire is the regional default for cattle perimeter and cross fence, and the least forgiving of a bad end assembly. Below is how we spec it and what we will ask you on the phone.

Where barbed wire is allowed near Spokane

Read this before you plan a residential line. It catches people out.

  • City of Spokane, residential zones. Barbed wire is permitted only in the RA (Residential Agriculture) zone, for agricultural use, and it is capped at three strands. Electric fence carries the same restriction: RA zone only, livestock only.
  • City of Spokane, commercial and industrial zones. Barbed wire is allowed, subject to the conditions in SMC 17C.
  • City of Spokane, any fence at all. The city requires a building permit for a fence regardless of height or construction material, including a replacement. Repairs are excepted.
  • Unincorporated county and other jurisdictions. The rules are different, and in several cases far looser. We confirm with the jurisdiction that governs your parcel before we set a post.

If your parcel sits inside city limits and is not zoned RA, barbed wire is not the answer. You will hear that on the phone, not after the material is on site. Field and woven wire is usually the fence you actually want.

Code language changes. We verify current requirements per job rather than working off a handout.

How many strands do you need?

Strand count is a livestock question first, a boundary question second.

Strands Typical use Notes
2 Marking a property line where nothing is being contained Marks the line. Holds nothing
3 Quiet cattle on adequate pasture. Also the maximum allowed for residential agricultural use inside the City of Spokane The regional default
4 Cattle under more pressure, mixed stock, smaller pastures, feed on the wrong side of the fence Tighter bottom spacing keeps calves in
5 Bulls, weaning pens, road frontage, high-pressure corners Closer spacing top to bottom, more posts, more brace

Strands are not spaced evenly. The bottom wires go closer together where calves push and where the pressure sits. Spacing opens up toward the top, where nothing leans.

Horses are a different problem entirely. Barbed wire is a poor horse fence at any strand count. A horse that hits wire keeps moving, and a barb holds the cut open. See horse and paddock fencing.

How far apart do the posts go?

Typical practice on flat, even ground is line posts every 12 to 16 feet, with stays between them at the wider spacings. That is a starting point, not a spec. Spacing tightens for:

  • Grade breaks. Every change in slope gets a post at the break. A strand should not bridge it.
  • Crests. Wire wants to lift off a rise. That post gets tied down, not just driven.
  • Dips and swales. Wire pulls up out of a low spot. That post has to resist uplift, which means a deeper set or an anchored post.
  • Curves. Tension pulls a curve straight. Posts go on the outside of the arc and they go closer together.
  • Wet ground, sand, and shallow soil. Holding power drops, so the post count goes up.

Spokane County ground is not uniform. Basalt shelf turns up at shallow depth across a lot of the county. Where we hit it we drill the hole rather than shift the post and pretend the line is straight.

Stays and droppers

A stay, also called a dropper or a batten, is a vertical spacer set between line posts. It holds the strands at their correct spacing so an animal cannot spread two wires apart and step through. It does not reach the ground and it does not carry tension.

Stays are the reason wider post spacing works. Two stays across a 16 foot span cost less than two more posts and hold the strand pattern just as well on ground that does not need the extra structure.

Where a stay is not a substitute for a post: grade breaks, crests, dips, curves, corners, gate openings, and anywhere stock puts steady pressure on the fence.

Corners, ends, and gate posts

This is the fence. Everything else is spacing.

Every strand you tension pulls against an end assembly. Line posts hold the wire at a height. Corners, ends, and gate posts hold the load, and they are where a barbed wire fence actually fails.

What we build:

  • H-brace at ends and corners. Two posts, a horizontal brace rail pinned between them, and a diagonal tension wire wrapped and twisted with a twitch stick so the diagonal pulls against the direction of pull.
  • Double H on long stretches, high strand counts, and soft ground.
  • A brace rail long enough to work. Trade practice runs the horizontal roughly two to two and a half times the height of the post above ground. A short brace levers the post out of the ground instead of resisting the pull.
  • Corner and end posts larger in diameter than line posts, and set deeper.
  • The diagonal twisted to tension, then checked again after the run is stretched.

A leaning corner post is not cosmetic. It is the fence telling you the brace geometry was wrong, and by the time the lean is visible, every strand in the run has already gone slack.

How tight should barbed wire be?

Barbed wire is soft, low carbon wire. It stretches under load and it stays stretched. So it is pulled to a moderate, even tension, not to its limit.

Over-tension it and you pull the wire past the point where it springs back. It will not recover after a cold snap or an animal hit, and you have dumped all of that load into the end assembly.

Under-tension it and you get the more common failure, which is worse than it looks:

  • Strands sag at the middle of every span, spacing collapses, and stock goes through at the loosest point.
  • The fence stops resisting. An animal leaning on slack wire moves it instead of being stopped by it.
  • Wind whips loose wire against the staple. That work-hardens the wire at one exact point, and that is where it snaps.
  • Snow and ice load drags a slack fence to the ground. A fence on the ground gets driven over and dragged.
  • Deer and elk crossing a loose line take the whole run down instead of stepping over it.

Wire gets stretched with a proper stretcher pulling against a braced end, one strand at a time, and it gets checked again after everything is stapled.

Staples, and how not to drive them

Two rules. The second is the one that gets missed.

Drive the staple at an angle across the grain. Driven square, both legs land in the same grain line and split the post. Rotate it so the legs enter different grain.

Do not drive the staple tight. The wire has to be able to move through it. A staple crushed down onto barbed wire clamps it, and a clamped strand cannot equalize tension from one span to the next. Every temperature swing, every animal hit, and every snow load then concentrates at that single point instead of spreading down the run. The wire work-hardens against the staple and breaks there. Splice it and it breaks in the same place again.

A staple holds the wire on the line of the fence. It is not a tensioning device and it is not a termination.

Terminations are separate. At an end post, a corner, or a gate post the wire is wrapped around the post and tied back on itself, so the post carries the load instead of the staple.

Wire spec

  • Gauge. 12.5 gauge, two strands twisted, is the working standard for permanent line.
  • Barbs. Four point holds better against cattle pressure. Two point does less damage. Barb spacing is commonly four or five inches.
  • Coating. Class 3 galvanized carries a heavier zinc coating than Class 1 and lasts considerably longer in wet ground and wet winters. It costs more per roll. On a fence you do not intend to touch for twenty years, it is the cheaper decision.

Whatever we quote, the class and the gauge are named on the quote in writing. If a bid does not say, ask.
