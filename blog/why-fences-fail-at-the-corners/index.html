Why fences fail at the corners

Outlaw Fence Co  ·  2026-09-05

Fences fail at the corners because the corner is the only place carrying the load. Line posts set height and spacing and carry almost nothing horizontal, while the corner takes the pull of every strand at once, and British Columbia's agriculture extension puts that pull at 1,000 to 1,500 lb normally and as much as 2,500 lb with cold weather contraction.

The University of Georgia's extension puts it higher: 3,000 lb when the wire is first stretched, 4,500 lb in winter cold. The two disagree, which is worth knowing before anyone quotes you one as settled fact. They agree on the shape of the problem. A corner holds thousands of pounds, all day, through every freeze.

There are only three ways it fails

A brace does not resist the wire by itself. It transfers load into the ground, so it is, in BC's words, "a combination of structure and soil, both are important." That gives three failure modes:

  • Structure failure. A broken post or a broken rail.
  • Soil failure. Soil shears in front of the post and the post moves.
  • End post pullout. The post comes out of the ground, which BC calls an indication of "poor brace design for the soil conditions."

Every corner you have seen laid over is one of those three, and which one tells you what went wrong. Pullout is not bad luck. It is geometry.

The load path, and why short braces pull out

The sequence, from the BC factsheet: wire tension bends the top of the end post inward, the rail passes that bend to the brace post, and the movement at the top of the brace post tensions the diagonal wire, which carries part of the load back down to the bottom of the end post. There the load splits in two, a horizontal force pushing the post through the soil and a vertical force pulling it out of the ground. The angle of the brace wire sets the ratio.

BC states the consequence plainly: "The steeper the angle of the brace wire, the greater this vertical pull-out force. This is why short span braces (with steeply angled brace wire) may fail at low fence loads by end post pullout."

A short brace fails at a lower load than a long one. Not the same load sooner. Lower. Hence the rail length rule:

A brace should be 2 1/2 times as long, post to post, as the height of the fence when the rail sits at fence height. Ten feet of brace for a four foot fence.

BC's substitute uses common post lengths: a rail 2 times the fence height, set at 3/4 of the fence height. A 2007 USDA specification for Alabama reaches the same ratio independently: "The brace member shall be at least 6 feet long or 2.5 times the height of the top wire."

If your brace rail is a 6 foot post on a 4 foot fence, nothing is wrong with your posts. The geometry is wrong, and it will let go early.

The corner that fails is usually the shallow one

A square corner is not the dangerous one. BC's second factsheet, on change-of-direction braces: "A change of direction produces reactive forces that are not inline with the brace that can cause leaning or complete brace failure. This is commonly seen at fence dog legs... Although a small change of direction produces a small force, a standard brace is not usually able to resist it. At a 90 degree corner, the brace is better able to resist it due to the alignment of brace members."

The small force at a dog leg beats the brace because it points somewhere the brace was never built to push back against. The bigger force at a square corner does not, because the brace members line up with it.

So the rules are geometric, not just "brace anything that turns." BC: under 60 degrees, use separate braced ends for each fence section, or brace the corner post itself; from 60 to 90 degrees, a standard shared-post corner brace works. The 2021 USDA Texas specification requires an assembly "where fence starts, stops, or has a change in direction of 20 degrees or more." Twenty degrees is the common threshold, though specifications vary: Alabama's requires braces "at all corners, gates, and angles up to 150 degrees."

These are state-level USDA specifications and university extension publications, not the national standard, and they do not all agree. Where they differ, both are shown rather than averaged.

Single H, double H, or floating

The decision rule is wire count. The 2021 Minnesota material specification: "Double H braces, corners, and pull assemblies will be used when six or more high tensile wires are strung on the fence. Single H braces, floating braces, and approved manufactured braces are approved for 2-5 wire fences."

BC agrees from the capacity side. In good soil a two-post assembly is adequate for six line wires or less, and three-post assemblies are required "in soils of low cohesive properties, such as sandy, very wet plastic clays or peats, or when using more than 6 line wires." A USDA job sheet adds two triggers for a double H: any 20 degree change in direction, and slope changes over 8 percent.

How much stronger is a double H? Nobody agrees. UGA says more than twice as strong, BC says almost double, and Stay-Tuff, a wire manufacturer, says 40 to 50 percent more. What is safe to say is that it is materially stronger and wire count is the trigger.

The floating brace is not a shortcut. The 2011 Washington specification: "Diagonal braces are structurally equal to the horizontal fence brace. The diagonal brace requires one less post, is 8% more resistant to overturn, 25% less expensive, and requires only about half the labor to install." Its weak point is that the diagonal wants to jack the end post out of the ground, and the cure is letting the foot of the diagonal move, which is what a slider block does. Michigan State's extension slides put its ceiling at a 5 wire high tensile fence, with the H brace good to 8.

On three-post assemblies, tie the fence wire off to the center post. BC: "Pulling from the center post of a two-post brace can actually re-align the posts whereas pulling from the rear post will cause buckling and failure."

The diagonal wire mistake

At a corner, run one diagonal, not two. The USDA Arkansas fact sheet puts the warning on the drawing: the twitch wire runs from the top of the fence-side post to the bottom of the pull-side post, and "DO NOT USE TWO diagonal twitch wires as the other wire pulls against the top of the pull post, defeating the purpose of the twitch wire." A separate USDA job sheet repeats that caption word for word, so it is two independent government documents, not one.

The exception is an inline brace mid-run, where each upright is a pull post and both diagonals do real work. Even there, BC warns the two "must not be wrapped together where they cross each other."

How tight is tight

Not as tight as you think. The Washington specification gives the only answer worth trusting, and it is not a number: "Only tighten until the top of the post is pushed back slightly." Tighten slowly, pulling up on the wire as you go to check the tension is even along its length, then tamp the soil around the post once more.

BC agrees: twist "to remove the wire slack," and the tension member "should not be over tightened, only enough to remove any slack," because "once the fence wires are tied off to the brace and tensioned, the brace will set." The fence tensions the brace. Your job at the stick is to take out slack, not to crank.

No source specifies a number of turns, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. The stick should be at least 1.5 by 1.5 inches, nailed to the rail so it cannot unwind, and it stays in the fence: the tightener "will remain in the wire after tightening is complete." That is what lets someone give it a half turn in year six instead of rebuilding the corner.

What a failing brace looks like first

The published sources give failure modes, not precursors. We found no extension or USDA document listing what a brace looks like in the months before it goes, so this list is Outlaw's own field observation:

  • The diagonal has gone slack, or the twitch stick has backed off.
  • A gap has opened at the base of the end post on the fence side, or the soil there is mounded.
  • The rail has started to bow, or the pin at the rail end is working proud.
  • The end post has picked up lean toward the pull, past what it was set with.
  • Staples on the first few line posts are backing out.

The sourced version is BC's: leaning at a dog leg is the visible stage of change-of-direction failure, and it comes before the failure.

Four build mistakes that show up years later

  • Do not attach fence wires to the brace assembly itself. Washington's specification says so in capitals.
  • Do not set brace posts in muck, peat, or soil where water stands. Same document. If you use concrete, it gets ten days to cure before any tension goes on the assembly.
  • Drive the posts in line with the pull. BC: "a small off-centre pull will tend to collapse the whole assembly."
  • Pre-load the corner. Lean the end post up to 2 inches away from the direction of pull, and about 1 inch on the center post of a three-post assembly.

For scale: 12.5 gauge smooth high tensile wire breaks at 1,300 lb, so five strands is 6,500 lb of wire behind one corner. The wire is not the weak link. The assembly is. How many strands you are actually pulling depends on what you are running, which is covered in how many strands of barbed wire you need.

All of this is agricultural wire fence engineering. The physics transfers to a residential wood or vinyl corner, but these dimensions and wire specs were not written for those fences.
