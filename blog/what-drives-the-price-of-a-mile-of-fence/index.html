What drives the price of a mile of fence

Outlaw Fence Co  ·  2026-09-05

A mile of fence gets quoted by the job instead of by the foot because the expensive parts are not spread evenly along the line. They sit at the corners, the ends, the gates, the rock, and the places where the ground rolls. Two ranches can each order a mile of five-strand barbed wire and get honestly different numbers, and the difference is almost never the wire.

Feet buy you posts and wire. Everything else on the quote is bought by geometry and ground.

The one published number, and what it leaves out

Iowa State University Extension publishes the fence budget most people end up quoting: Ag Decision Maker B1-75, revised December 2024. It prices a 1,320 ft (quarter mile) straight perimeter fence at 2024 Iowa retail material prices, with labor valued at $20.00 per hour, taken from surveyed Iowa custom fence-building charges that "include the cost of equipment and tools for building fence, as well as labor."

Read that again. Straight. Quarter mile. Iowa prices, Iowa's labor rate. It is the best public reference there is, and it is the easiest possible geometry priced in another state's economy. It is not our pricing and it is not an Idaho number.

That last column is the one ranchers underuse. Electrified high-tensile is the cheapest to build and the cheapest to own, $0.16 per foot per year against woven wire's $0.40.

Multiply Iowa State's straight quarter mile by four and a straight mile of their barbed wire comes to $10,519.20. That multiplication is ours, not theirs, and it is a floor rather than an estimate: it carries the two end braces Iowa State priced, no corners, no gates, no rock, no brush, no grade. Braces are the expensive part, and corners are where they live

NRAES-11, the Cooperative Extension high-tensile fencing manual, states the mechanism plainly: "Because wire strands are stretched to maintain up to 300 pounds of tension, depending on wire gauge, corner and end posts must be sturdy and well-braced. Line posts are smaller than corner and end posts because they mainly serve to hold up the wire and maintain wire spacing."

A line post holds wire up. A corner holds the fence together, and that shows up in the bill of materials before it ever shows up in labor. From NRAES-11's specification table, a corner or gate post is 6 to 7 inches in diameter, 8 feet long, set 4 feet deep. A line post is 4 inches, 6.5 feet long, set 2.5 feet deep. The corner also needs a second or third post, a horizontal rail, galvanized brace pins, a half-inch brace rod, and double-wrapped 12.5-gauge brace wire on a strainer, plus a bigger auger to put it in.

Iowa State prices the same gap in dollars. An 8-inch brace post is $35.00, a 4-inch wood line post is $14.00, a steel T-post is $6.00. A brace post costs 2.5 times a line post and 5.83 times a T-post, before the rail, rod, pins and extra hours.

Now count assemblies. Iowa State's budget has two ends and zero corners, which is why its woven-wire table carries four 8-inch posts (two per end) and its high-tensile tables carry six (three per end). Add one corner and you have added a third assembly to a job priced for two. Add a gate opening and you have added two more heavy posts, since NRAES-11 specs gate posts identically to corner posts. Red Brand describes its own U-brace accessory as a steel brace that "supports wooden corner and end posts."

Even the post count inside one assembly is a judgment call. NRAES-11 notes that a three-post, two-rail double brace is recommended for longer runs of ten-strand fence, while a two-post, one-rail single brace "may be adequate for eight-strand or less in heavy soil." Same end, two different bills, decided by strand count and dirt. More on the failure mode in why fences fail at the corners, and on assembly types in braces and corners.

Rolling ground adds posts, and rock changes the assembly

Maximum post spacing is a ceiling, not a schedule. NRAES-11 repeatedly requires line posts on all rises and all depressions regardless of the maximum, with instructions like "install line posts on all rises or at a maximum of 150' apart on smooth ground." A fence has to be held down in a swale and held up over a crest, or the wire lifts and the stock walks under it. Post count on rolling ground is set by the shape of the ground, not by dividing feet by spacing. We will not hand you a percentage for it, because no source we opened publishes one. That rule is why two miles on the same ranch price differently.

Rock does more than slow the auger. NRAES-11's Figure 3 is captioned "Diagonal Brace Assembly for Ends and Corners Preferred in Rocky or Hard Soil," and it is a different build: a 6-inch pressure-treated post set 48 to 60 inches deep, a 4-inch by 10-foot brace post, a 3/8 by 10-inch galvanized brace pin, a half-inch brace rod, and a reinforced concrete brace block, 4 by 8 by 18 inches. Rock substitutes a concrete-footed assembly for a standard one, which is a materials change and not just an hours change. How the hole gets made matters too: NRAES-11 notes that "driven posts have initially about five times the holding strength of hand-set posts."

Penn State Extension names the same planning variables: topography, depth to bedrock, and the location of gates and lanes for equipment access. No numbers attached, and we will not attach any either. Brush clearing and haul distance work the same way. They move the hours, and we found no published figure that prices them honestly, so ours land in the walkthrough as [PRICE_TBD] rather than as an invented multiplier.

Wire type, gauge and coating are three separate levers

"12.5 gauge" is a diameter, not a spec. Red Brand's catalog lists high-tensile smooth wire at 200,000 PSI in 12.5 gauge alongside commercial galvanized wire in the same 12.5 gauge at roughly the same weight per foot. Near-identical on a tape measure, entirely different product and price. Gauge also reads backwards, in the manufacturer's own words: "The larger the number, the smaller the wire."

Coating is a third lever. Red Brand's minimum coating weights are Class 3 at 0.80 oz/ft2, Class 1 at 0.28 oz/ft2, commercial at none. Class 3 carries about 2.9 times the zinc, which is why two spools of the same gauge get quoted far apart, and why we name the class instead of burying it.

Idaho law sets a floor under all of it. Idaho Code 35-102(4) describes a lawful wire fence as posts "not more than twenty-four feet (24') apart," with "three (3) substantial stays" between them, and "not less than three (3) barbed wires, or four (4) coiled spring wires of not less than number nine (9) gauge," bottom wire no more than 21 inches off the ground, fence not less than 47 inches high. Then it offers a trade: "provided, that if barbed wire only is used, and the posts are not more than sixteen feet (16') apart, no stays need be used." That is a legally defined swap of post count against stay count, and a real budget decision. One caution: the general section, 35-101, sets 4.5 feet and a 20-inch bottom wire, reconciling with 35-102 only through its own "except as hereinafter provided" clause. Never take an Idaho height quote that does not name its section.

Gates sit outside the per-foot number entirely

Iowa State says it in six words: "Gates are not included in the estimates." Every per-foot figure in the table above is a gate-free figure.

Idaho law is no help either. Idaho Code 35-112, "Establishment of gates," is entirely about right of passage and liability for leaving a gate open, with double damages to the aggrieved party. It sets no width, no count and no spec.

What drives gate cost is your equipment and your stock flow. Every opening is two braced posts plus hanging hardware, and a wide opening for a baler is a different structure than a walk-through. See ranch gates and entries for how we spec them.

A boundary run and a paddock system are not the same job

Red Brand's acreage diagram makes the geometry concrete: a 40-acre square is a quarter mile on a side and "requires 1 mile of fence to enclose." We checked it. A 1,320 ft square is 1,742,400 sq ft, exactly 40.0 acres, perimeter 5,280 ft.

Now the scaling. Red Brand's acreage table gives 16 rolls of 330 ft field fence to enclose 40 acres and 51 rolls to enclose 400 acres. Ten times the ground, 3.19 times the fence. Perimeter grows with the square root of area. The table also assumes the best case, since Red Brand calls these "suggested guidelines assuming the square area to be fenced." Any irregular boundary needs more fence and more corners for the same acres.

Cross-fencing inverts that math. Interior fence encloses nothing new, it adds corners, and corners are the expensive assemblies. Iowa State flags it in its opening paragraph: "the shape of the paddocks affects the amount of materials needed and labor required for construction of the fence."

Iowa State also publishes one clean materials-versus-labor trade: on non-electric high-tensile, setting posts 30 feet apart instead of 20 and adding two stay rods between each pair would reduce cost "about $220 for every 1,320 feet of fence, or $0.166 per foot." Fewer holes, more hardware.

One more Idaho item for boundary runs: sections 35-103 through 35-106 cover partition fences between adjoining landowners, including how disagreements get resolved by viewers. Who pays for a shared line is a statutory question here, not just a neighborly one.
