How to read a woven wire fence number

Outlaw Fence Co  ·  2026-09-05

A woven wire fence number like 1047-6-12½ reads left to right as ten horizontal line wires, 47 inches of fabric height, 6 inches between the vertical stay wires, and 12½ gauge wire. Red Brand publishes that decode itself, under the heading "How to read fence style numbers," and breaks the same example into four labeled lines: "10 #Line wires," "47 Height in inches," "6 Pullout," "12½ Wire gauge."

That is the whole trick. The rest of this page is the part nobody tells you: which of those four numbers means something different depending on who made the roll, what the number leaves out entirely, and how to pick the right one for what you are running.

The first two numbers are the honest ones

Line wires and height move together, and they are not two independent choices. Red Brand's field fence spacing illustration marks 26, 32, 39 and 47 inches as height stops on a single spacing ladder, which means a shorter field fence is not a different geometry. It is the same ladder cut off earlier. Seven wires gets you 26 inches, eight gets 32, nine gets 39, ten gets 47.

That is not just a diagram reading. Stay-Tuff sells a real 726-6-330: seven horizontal wires, 26 inch height, 6 inch vertical spacing. Red Brand's non-climb horse line does the same thing at 13 wires and 48 inches, 16 and 60, 19 and 72. Stay-Tuff's tallest fixed knot product, ST 2096-6-330, is 20 wires at 96 inches. The pairing holds from knee height to eight feet. The count includes the top and bottom wire.

The middle number is the one that decides the animal

Red Brand calls it "pull-out" and defines it plainly: "Pull-out is the measure, in inches, of the distance between the vertical stay wires." Stay-Tuff's spec column says "Vertical Spacing." Its anatomy diagram calls the wire itself a "Stay Wire (Vertical Wire)." Three names, one number.

The cleanest example of what it does: Red Brand sells 1348-4-12½ as sheep and goat fence and 1348-2-12½ as non-climb horse fence. Identical wire count, height and gauge. The only thing that differs is the middle digit, 4 inches against 2, and that single digit changes which animal the fence is built for.

The third number is where people get burned

On a Red Brand roll, the last number is wire gauge. On a Stay-Tuff roll, it is roll length in feet. ST 949-6-330 is nine wires, 49 inches, 6 inch spacing, 330 foot roll. ST 1348-12-660 is a 660 foot roll of the same idea.

Stay-Tuff's hinge joint table lists two different part numbers carrying the identical designation 939-6-330, one at 12.5 gauge line wire with 10.5 top and bottom, the other at 14 gauge line with 12.5 top and bottom. Same designation, different wire. If the third number were gauge, that could not happen. Read the gauge out of the spec column, not out of the name.

Two manufacturers, two conventions for the same slot. Anybody who tells you the fourth position is an industry standard has not compared the two catalogs.

Gauge: smaller number, bigger wire

Red Brand says it in one line: "The larger the number, the smaller the wire." Its chart runs 20, 18, 17, 16, 14½, 14, 12½, 11, 10, 9, drawn as bars that get fatter as the number drops. A 9 gauge wire is heavy. A 14 is light.

Two things the designation does not tell you.

The gauge in the name is the filler gauge only. Red Brand's spec tables carry two columns, "Filler Gauge" and "Top & Bottom Gauge," and the top and bottom wires are always heavier. On 1047-6-11 the filler is 11 and the top and bottom are 9. On both 939-6-12½ and 1047-6-12½ the filler is 12½ and the top and bottom are 10. That heavier top wire is the one that takes the pressure of an animal leaning, and it is not in the style number.

Gauge is not strength. University of Georgia Extension puts it directly: mesh fencing for horses "should have a minimum of 12.5 gauge wire unless it is high-tensile steel. A 14-gauge high-tensile steel wire will provide more strength than a 12.5 gauge standard steel wire." A thinner high-tensile wire beats a thicker low carbon one. If two bids quote different gauges, the wire type has to be part of that comparison or it means nothing.

Why the small openings go at the bottom

Look at the chart. Those nine numbers are the vertical gaps in Red Brand's field fence ladder, read from the ground up: 3, 3½, 4, 4½, 5, 5½, 6, 7, 8 inches. The openings more than double between the bottom wire and the top. Stay-Tuff's profiles do the same thing. Its 12 inch "livestock, no predator control" profile runs 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7 from the bottom, eight gaps summing to 49 inches over nine line wires, which is a 949.

The documented reason for the tight bottom is predators, and the manufacturer labels it that way. Stay-Tuff calls its 3 inch stay option "Predator Proof, 3 by 3 at bottom of fence" and the 6 inch option "Predator Control, 3 by 6 at bottom of fence." For sheep and goats there is a second reason: Red Brand says its 4 by 4 mesh "provides strong confinement and deters animals from putting their heads through the fence," which is the horn and head entrapment problem.

If digging under is the real worry, Stay-Tuff's advice is to add wire rather than only tighten mesh: run a high-tensile Class 3 galvanized barbed strand along the bottom, top, or both. Its footnote belongs next to any spacing table: "Spacing between Vertical Wire (stays) is nominal and may vary slightly from the examples shown."

Hinge joint and fixed knot are not in the number either

Red Brand sells 939-6-12½ and 1047-6-12½ in both its Monarch knot and its Square Deal knot. Same designations, different construction. The style number tells you nothing about how the fabric is tied together, and the knot is arguably the biggest thing about it.

The two designs have different stated intents. Red Brand on its hinge-type knot: "The Monarch Knot acts as a hinge that gives under pressure, then allows the fence to spring back into shape." Stay-Tuff on the fixed knot: "The Fixed Knot resists slipping vertically or horizontally. Solid vertical Stay Wires resist bending or folding."

Read those as two design philosophies, not as a scoreboard. No manufacturer publishes a head to head test, and the common shorthand that fixed knot means high tensile while hinge joint does not is simply false: Stay-Tuff sells high tensile hinge joint fence with Class 3 galvanization under the same warranty as its fixed knot line.

What you buy with a fixed knot system is a specific set of properties: high tensile wire, solid stays that resist bending, a knot that will not slide along the line wire, and the tension crimp that lets the fence be pulled up and adjusted. Stay-Tuff's install instruction is to "continue tensioning until approximately 50% of the crimp in the line is removed." In that system the braces at each end carry the whole load and the line posts "are intended only to hold the fence wire vertically in place," which is why braces and corners decide whether the fence survives. We will not put a premium figure on fixed knot here, because no manufacturer publishes one. Ask for both on your bid and compare the real numbers.

Galvanizing is not in the number, and it decides the lifespan

Coating class rides on the product family, not the style number. Red Brand's galvanization guide maps it: Tradition is Class 1, Extended Life is Class 3, High Tensile is Class 3, Sierra is commercial galvanized. Two rolls with the identical designation can carry different coating.

Red Brand publishes the minimum coating weights, based on 12½ gauge wire: Class 3 at .80 oz per square foot, Class 1 at .28, commercial minimal. It claims Class 3 gives "2½ times longer service life than Class 1 coating." Stay-Tuff claims three times the corrosion resistance. Take it as a range of roughly 2.5x to 3x rather than a settled number, since the two makers disagree. University of Georgia Extension puts it in years: "Class 3 galvanizing can easily add 5 to 10 years of life to fence wire in a humid climate like Georgia's."

Stay-Tuff backs its Class 3 coating with a 20 year limited warranty covering fixed knot, stiff stay, hinge joint, electric wire and barbed wire alike, voided for damage within 20 miles of the coast. That is a warranty term, not a measured lifespan, but it tells you where the manufacturer will put money.

Which designation for which animal

Red Brand publishes an effectiveness matrix scoring nine fence families across ten animal types, 5 being most effective. It is blunt about where field fence does and does not belong.

Fence Cattle Equine Sheep Goat Swine
Field fence (939, 1047 family) 5 1 2 1 3
Sheep and goat (1348-4) 3 3 5 5 4
Non-climb horse (1348-2) 3 5 3 3 3
Keepsafe V-mesh horse 3 5 3 3 3
Panels 5 5 5 5 5

Cattle. The 939 and 1047 field fence family, which Red Brand calls "our most reliable confinement for cattle, hogs and other large animals." Stay-Tuff's cattle line is a 949 at 6, 12 or 3 inch spacing. Twelve inch stays are fine here, because a cow is not putting a foot through a twelve inch gap. More on this at field and woven wire.

Horses. Two inch stay spacing. Red Brand's reason: "2 by 4 spacing prevents stepping through and climbing up," and it recommends a sight rail on equine jobs. Stay-Tuff's horse line runs 1661 and 1748, with 3 inch spacing for stalls and holding pens where pawing is the problem and 6 inch for pasture. Field fence scores a 1 for horses in Red Brand's own matrix. See horse and paddock.

Sheep and goats. Red Brand's 1348-4-12½ is uniform 4 inch spacing, twelve gaps over 48 inches, not graduated. Stay-Tuff's goat line is a 1348 at 12 inch spacing for the economical build or 3 inch "for complete predator control." The mesh has to be tight enough that horns do not go through it.

Red Brand's framing above that matrix is the right closing thought: "Imagine what purposes you may have in the future before selecting the type of fence you plan to install."

What to ask before you buy a roll

Four questions cover it. What is the designation, and does this maker use gauge or roll length in the last slot? What is the top and bottom wire gauge, not just the filler? Is the wire high tensile or low carbon? Is the coating Class 1 or Class 3?

None of that is on the tag in full, and all of it is in the manufacturer's spec table. One more thing the tag will not tell you: a sheet of woven wire loads a corner far harder than a few strands of barbed do, which is why the roll you pick changes the bracing. That is covered in why fences fail at the corners.
