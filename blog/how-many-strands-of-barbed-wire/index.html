How many strands of barbed wire do you need?

Outlaw Fence Co  ·  2026-09-05

Three or four strands hold cattle on most pasture. Five is a weaning-pen and high-pressure answer, not a default, and horses should not be behind barbed wire at all.

Most of the time that is the whole answer, because strand count is the least important number here. Where the wires sit off the ground decides whether calves get stranded, whether elk clear the top, and whether what you built is a lawful fence under Washington law.

Strand count follows pressure, not tradition

The Wyoming Game and Fish Department's handbook is blunt about the reflex to overbuild: "Most livestock pastures do not require a 5- to 6-strand barbed wire fence," and a three or four strand fence works well "particularly if the pasture quality inside the fence is as good or better as outside the fence."

That last clause is the real variable. Feed on the wrong side of the wire is what pushes stock through a fence, and no strand count fixes a grazing problem. The same handbook names the classes that justify more: "Sheep, bison, and cows with calves may require a more impermeable fence for control." Past that the published guidance runs out, and it is worth saying so rather than filling the gap.

Cow-calf. Montana State University Extension in Judith Basin County, writing about fence-line weaning, says "a typical five-strand barbed wire fence will usually be satisfactory," and if calves still nurse through it, add an offset electric strand. Note the context. That is a weaning fence with pairs pushing from both sides, the hardest thing a cattle fence ever does, not a claim that every cow-calf pasture needs five wires.

Yearlings, and mixed classes. No extension or agency source we could open gives a strand count for yearlings, or for classes run together. The only published mixed design is Wyoming's sheep-and-cattle column at 10, 18, 26 and 38 inches. Anyone quoting you a yearling number is quoting experience, not a spec.

Bulls. The only controlled evidence in the literature is not barbed wire at all: a three-wire high-tensile electric fence at 22, 32 and 40 inches "has been shown to contain bulls separated from cows coming into estrus, and calves from cows in the fall" (Karhu and Anderson 2003, 2006; Quitmeyer et al. 2004). That is an electric spec and it does not convert into a barbed wire strand count. To hold a bull off cycling cows, high tensile with a hot wire is the tool with evidence behind it.

Horses. Not barbed wire, and this is not a judgment call. Rutgers Cooperative Extension: "Barbed wire fencing is not acceptable for horses due to its potential for serious injury. Horses fight harder than other livestock if trapped in a fence." Penn State Extension notes that owners "will relay stories about the savings for cheaper, but unsafe, horse fence (barbed wire, for example) eventually being paid for in veterinary bills." Bekaert's own catalog lists horse, llama, alpaca, sheep, goat, ostrich, emu, deer or elk, and swine as species it does not recommend barbed wire for. Horse options are on the horse and paddock page.

That list rules out sheep, while the Wyoming handbook publishes a four-strand barbed design explicitly for cattle or sheep. The sources conflict on sheep. They agree on horses.

Where the wires go

Read that as heights measured up from the ground, not gaps between wires. The gaps fall out of it: 18 inches of air under the bottom wire, then 4, then 4, then a full 12 inches to the top.

The top wire. The 2025 revision tightened the old rule of thumb to a 40 inch maximum with 38 inches encouraged, and says why: "Although a 42 inch top has long been recommended for wildlife friendlier fence, research now shows a 40 inch is better." If you learned 42, that guidance has moved.

The top two wires. Keep them at least 12 inches apart, because "deer and elk easily tangle their back legs if the top wires are closer" together. The mortality data is not subtle: juveniles are eight times more likely to die in a fence than adults, and 70 percent of all mortalities were on fences higher than 40 inches.

The bottom wire, and why it is about calves getting back

Set the bottom wire "a minimum of 16 inches from the ground and preferably at least 18," per Montana FWP. Most ranchers hear that and picture calves walking out. The published rationale is the opposite: "Many cattle ranchers have found that although a small calf may slip under the higher bottom wire, it can also easily slip back" to its mother.

The risk being managed is a stranded calf, not an escaped one. A calf that goes under a 12 inch wire and cannot find its way back is the expensive outcome. A calf that ducks an 18 inch wire and ducks back twenty minutes later costs nothing.

Wildlife uses the same clearance. The focal-species table in the Wyoming handbook, adapted from Hamilton et al. (2023), lands on that same 18 inch bottom gap for mule deer, pronghorn, elk, bighorn sheep and moose, with elk wanting 20 inches or more. Pronghorn go under rather than over, which is why the bottom wire is barbless in all of those designs.

What Washington and Idaho actually require

This is the part that catches people, and the reason we ask which side of the state line your ground is on.

Three of those statutes are satisfied by three wires or say nothing about spacing. Washington is the outlier, and its definition is exact: "at least four barbed, horizontal, well-stretched wires, spaced so that the top wire is forty-eight inches, plus or minus four inches, above the ground and the other wires at intervals below the top wire of twelve, twenty-two, and thirty-two inches."

A 38 inch wildlife-friendly top wire sits below Washington's floor of 44 inches and nowhere near Idaho's 54 inch minimum. That conflict matters because of what lawful-fence status buys you. Under RCW 16.60.015 a person maintaining a lawful fence "may recover in a suit for trespass... from the owner or owners of any animal or animals which shall break through such fence, in full for all damages sustained," and RCW 16.60.076 makes the proof simple: "it shall be sufficient to prove that the fence was lawful when the break was made." Build below the statutory spec and you have broken no law, but you have given up a statutory remedy against your neighbor's stock.

One thing does reconcile. Washington's tolerance runs to 52 inches on the top wire, and the fixed 32 inch interval below it puts the bottom wire at 20 inches. That is the most ground clearance a lawful Washington fence can have, it is exactly Idaho's ceiling for a bottom wire, and it clears the 18 inches the wildlife guidance asks for. The top wire cannot be reconciled. The bottom wire can, and it is the one that does the most good.

None of this is legal advice, and no county or municipal ordinance is reflected above. Check your own jurisdiction.

Post spacing and stays

For four or five strands, the extension figure is line posts at 12 or 16 feet. Washington sets a ceiling and ties stays directly to it: posts "not more than twenty-four feet apart," and "if the posts are set more than sixteen feet apart, the wires shall be supported by stays placed no more than eight feet from each other or from the posts."

That collides with current wildlife guidance, which is against wire stays: "No vertical stays. If used, consider stiff wood, plastic, or composite stays." Montana FWP is specific about why, calling easily bent wire stays a hazard because wildlife bend them.

The resolution is simple. Keep line posts at 16 feet or less and the stay question never comes up. Set corner posts about 3 to 3.5 feet deep and brace posts at least 3 feet, in a dug and well-tamped hole rather than driven. Everything the fence does under tension comes back to the end assemblies, which is the whole subject of braces and corners.

What changes on rolling ground

Two things change, and neither is the strand count.

Effective height goes up with slope. A 40 inch fence on level ground is a 46.6 inch obstacle at 10 percent slope, 60 inches at 30 percent and 73 inches at 50 percent, because an animal jumping uphill has less room than the tape measure suggests. Montana FWP runs the same math off a 42 inch base and gets 48.6, 62 and 75 inches. "Fences on steep slopes become nearly impossible for animals to jump without injury."

Braces double up. Level land takes one line brace every 40 rods, 660 feet. Hilly land takes one every 20 rods, 330 feet. Note that is a bracing number, not a post-spacing number. If somebody hands you a rule like "tighten line posts to 10 feet on slopes," that is a shop rule, not a published spec.

Suspension fence is the other rolling-ground tool: no more than four strands, anchor posts 50 to 100 feet apart "or much closer in uneven terrain," with enough bracing to hold tension. And work the topography instead of fighting it. Montana FWP says to use small dips, swales and gullies to give a slightly larger gap under the bottom wire, which turns your low spots into crossings rather than the places the fence gets wrecked.

One note on the wire itself

A lower gauge number is not automatically stronger wire. Bekaert's own strength ranking puts 15.5 gauge Gaucho high-strength above 12.5 gauge low-carbon Classic. Gauge is diameter, strength is metallurgy, and whichever you buy, hang it from the top down: "always attach the top wire first... this will assure that all wires are of equal tension."

The short version

Three or four strands for most cattle pasture. Five where you are weaning or fighting real pressure. Never barbed wire for horses. Put the top wire at 38 to 40 inches if wildlife passage matters more than lawful-fence status, or at 44 to 52 inches if it does not, and know which one you picked. Keep 12 inches between the top two wires, run the bottom wire barbless at 18 to 20 inches, hold line posts at 16 feet or under, and double the braces on hills.
