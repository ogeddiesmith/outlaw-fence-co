What makes a fence last on acreage

Nothing in the middle of the run. The wire and the line posts are the cheap part and they are not what fails. A wire fence fails at the ends, at the corners, at the gates and in the low spots. That is where the money should go, and it is the first place a cheap bid takes it out.

Bracing

Every end, every corner, every gate post and every place the line changes direction gets a braced assembly. An H-brace is two posts, a horizontal rail pinned between them, and a diagonal brace wire twisted tight from the base of the end post to the top of the brace post.

The rail has to be long enough for the height of the fence. A short brace levers the end post out of the ground instead of holding it, and that is what you are looking at when a corner leans in toward the field two years after it went up. Long runs and heavy tension get a double H. Corners get braced both directions, because a corner post takes the pull of every strand on two lines at once.

The full detail on assemblies, rail length, twitch wire and post sizing is on braces and corners.

Tension

Wire holds an animal because it is tight, not because it is there. Barbed and woven wire get stretched against a braced end with a stretcher, not pulled by hand and stapled. Staples get driven at an angle across the grain and are not sunk hard onto a wire that has to move.

High tensile is a different system. The load lives in the wire, so it gets in-line strainers, tension springs and a working tension set on purpose. Wire contracts in cold and slacks in heat. That is what the springs absorb, and it is why a fence pulled drum-tight on a hot August afternoon is a fence that snaps strands in January.

Posts: depth, diameter, driven or set

Line posts and brace posts are not the same post and should not be priced the same way.

A driven post holds better in wire fence than a post in a hole, because the ground around it was never disturbed. A post in an augered hole is only as good as the backfill, and in freeze and thaw ground a concrete collar gives frost something to grab and lift. So: driven where the soil allows it, augered and set where rock, shallow soil or a gate post that has to carry weight for twenty years says otherwise.

Everything goes below frost depth for the jurisdiction it is in. Corners and ends go deeper than line posts and take a larger diameter post. Diameter matters as much as depth at the ends.

Corners and gate posts

A corner is the highest load point in the fence. A gate post is harder. It carries a dead weight hanging off one side all day, it gets swung, and it gets slammed. Gate posts go bigger, go deeper and get braced back into the line.

A 12 foot tube gate hung on an undersized, unbraced post will drag within a couple of seasons. Then it gets left open. Then the fence is decorative.

The ground itself

Wire wants to lift out of a low spot and press down over a rise. Draws and dips get a tie-down or a deadman anchor so stock cannot push under. Crests get an extra post. Creek and draw crossings get a floodgate arrangement that lets go instead of taking a hundred feet of fence out with the first high water.

Rock is a price and a schedule item, not a surprise. Spokane County sits on basalt, and whether your line hits it changes the job. If you or a neighbor have hit rock on that ground, tell us on the first call.
